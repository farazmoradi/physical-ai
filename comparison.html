Five startups teaching robots to work, side by side

"Physical AI" just means AI that controls things in the real world, like robot arms in a factory, instead of AI that only writes text or makes pictures. These five young companies all want to make robots more useful, but each one works on a different piece of the puzzle. This page shows who does what, in plain words.

First, the big picture: how does a robot learn a job?

Think of it like training a new employee. Each step below is a place where a company can help.

1. Show examplesCollect videos or recordings of the job being done.
2. Practice in a video gameLet the robot try millions of times in a computer copy of the world, where mistakes cost nothing.
3. Teach the robotTurn all that practice into the robot's "brain", the software that decides what it does.
4. Give it an examCheck it actually works before trusting it with the real thing.
5. Put it to workInstall the robot in a real factory or warehouse.
6. Keep an eye on thingsWatch the machines once they're running and catch problems early.

Who are their customers, and where does the robot learn?

This is my rough judgment from public info, not exact data. Hover over or tap a dot to see a one-line summary.

Left side: they sell to robot makers. Veeda, Kite and WorldEngine don't build robots. They sell the "school supplies" that robot companies use to train their robots.

Top vs. bottom. Veeda and Kite think robots should mostly practice in a computer copy of the world (cheap and safe). WorldEngine thinks real-life experience is what robots are missing.

Right side: they sell to factories. Shiraz brings the robot itself. KavAI brings software that watches a factory's existing machines.
